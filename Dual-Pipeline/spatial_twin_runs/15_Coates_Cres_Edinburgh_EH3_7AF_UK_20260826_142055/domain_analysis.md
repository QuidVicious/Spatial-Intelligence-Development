# Spatial Domain Analysis: 15 Coates Cres, Edinburgh EH3 7AF, UK

**View Scope:** FRUSTUM

## 1. Geology & Indigenous Lithics
The underlying subterranean bedrock of this Edinburgh West End sector comprises Carboniferous sedimentary strata of the Gullane Formation (Strathclyde Group), composed of alternating sandstones, siltstones, and mudstones overlain by glacial till deposits. The monumental masonry of St Mary's Episcopal Cathedral and the surrounding Georgian/Victorian residential tenements is built primarily of indigenous Carboniferous quartz arenite sandstone sourced from local Edinburgh quarries, notably the fine-grained, siliceous, hard-wearing Craigleith Quarry "liver rock", alongside secondary stones from Hailes and Ravelston quarries. Mortar chemistry consists of traditional historic non-hydraulic and semi-hydraulic hot-mixed slaked lime mortars blended with local sharp siliceous aggregate, facilitating vapor permeability across the sandstone fabric. Natural subterranean drainage flows northwards down structural gradients toward the Water of Leith basin.

## 2. Architecture & Planar Rectification
- **Centerpiece Structure (Midground):** St Mary’s Episcopal Cathedral (Category A listed; designed by Sir George Gilbert Scott, 1874–1879). High Victorian Gothic Revival style featuring a dominant 90-meter central crossing spire and twin western spires (completed 1913–1917). Planar elements include steep pitched nave and transept roofs clad in Westmorland/Ballachulish slate, clerestory lancets, deeply recessed portal jambs, flying buttresses, intricate stone crockets, gable copings, and a prominent decorative rose window in the south transept.
- **Foreground & Midground Residential Blocks:** 4- to 5-storey Georgian and Victorian West End tenements (Coates Crescent / Palmerston Place / Melville Street area). Planar facades are strictly vertical, featuring classic regular fenestration grids with double-hung vertical timber sash-and-case windows (typically 6-over-6 and 2-over-2 patterns), heavy rusticated sandstone basements, dressed ashlar stringcourses, dentiled eaves cornices, pitched double-mansard slate roofs, and pronounced rectilinear sandstone chimney stacks topped with cylindrical clay chimney pots.
- **Planar Rectification & Anti-Warp Protocol:** All photogrammetry melting, wobbly vertical faces, and distorted roof planes are rectified into true gravity verticals, sharp roof ridges, rectilinear dormers, and plumb ashlar joint lines.

## 3. Materials & Environmental Patina
- **St Mary's Cathedral:** Honed and dressed Craigleith and Hailes sandstone ashlar, exhibiting natural historic carbon-soot deposition and atmospheric sulfate crusting in sheltered decorative reveals, contrasting against rain-washed, pale buff-gray upper buttress faces. Roofs are clad in dark blue-gray Welsh/Scottish roofing slates with lead ridge rolls, copper flashing patinated to a soft dull gray-green, and wrought iron finials.
- **Tenement Blocks:** Golden-buff to muted ash-gray Craigleith sandstone ashlar facades, showing authentic Scottish temperate maritime weathering: gypsum crusting beneath projecting cornices, gentle damp-induced stone discoloration, softened lime-pointed mortar joints, lead-lined roof valleys, and dark cast-iron rainwater downpipes and guttering.

## 4. Landscape Ecology & Botanical Canopy
The urban canopy dispersed within the private residential gardens and cathedral precinct consists of mature temperate deciduous species typical of Edinburgh's historic parks and streetscapes:
- European Ash (*Fraxinus excelsior*)
- Sycamore Maple (*Acer pseudoplatanus*)
- Wych Elm (*Ulmus glabra*)
- London Plane (*Platanus × acerifolia*)
- European Lime (*Tilia × europaea*)
Canopies feature dense, organic leafy branch structures with varied foliage tones ranging from deep forest green to lighter lime green, distinctly differentiated from surrounding stone masonry.
