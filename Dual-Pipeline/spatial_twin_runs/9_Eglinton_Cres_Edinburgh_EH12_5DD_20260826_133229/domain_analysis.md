# Spatial Domain Analysis: 9 Eglinton Cres, Edinburgh EH12 5DD, UK

**View Scope:** FRUSTUM

## 1. Geology & Indigenous Lithics
The subterranean strata beneath Edinburgh's West End consist of early Carboniferous sedimentary sequences of the Strathclyde Group (sandstones, siltstones, and thin mudstones/shales), uncomformably overlying volcanic intrusions and basaltic plugs such as those forming Castle Rock and Arthur's Seat visible on the eastern horizon. The primary construction material defining the entire vista is indigenous Carboniferous Craigleith and Hailes sandstone—a quartz-rich, pale greyish-buff to light olive-brown sandstone known for exceptional density and silica cementation. Mortar beds throughout the Victorian and late Georgian ashlar construction are formulated from traditional non-hydraulic to weakly hydraulic lime mortar aggregate mixtures. Subterranean moisture management relies on natural subterranean gradients draining towards the Water of Leith basin, with extensive subterranean basement lightwells (areas) lined with droved stone retaining walls.

## 2. Architecture & Planar Rectification
The central visual anchor is St Mary's Episcopal Cathedral (designed by Sir George Gilbert Scott, consecrated 1879), an Early English/Gothic Revival cruciform cathedral featuring a towering 90m central crossing spire with decorative broaches, paired 84m western flanking towers ("Barbara" and "Mary", completed 1917), steeply pitched slate rooflines, clear cross-gabled transepts, flying buttresses, and tall lancet clerestory windows. Surrounding the cathedral is the high-density Victorian and late Georgian West End residential/civic grid, predominantly 3-to-4 storey plus raised basement and attic dormer terraced tenements and crescent townhouses (e.g., Palmerston Place, Eglinton Crescent). All photogrammetry distortion is rectified: wall planes are plumbed to absolute gravity-vertical; long continuous slate mansards, chimneys stacks, stone balustrades, string courses, and ashlar cornices form rigid, planar linear horizontals.

## 3. Materials & Environmental Patina
- **Cathedral**: Squared, finely drafted and rock-faced Craigleith sandstone ashlar with intricately carved stone capitals, mouldings, and traceries. Material pathology shows authentic maritime-temperate weathering: historic airborne soot deposition concentrated in recessed mouldings and under cornices, subtle vertical rainwater run-off leaching across ashlar faces, and dark Scottish slate roofing tiles.
- **Surrounding Terraces & Tenements**: Polished and channelled buff sandstone ashlar on front facades, coursed rubble masonry to secondary/rear elevations, Scottish blue-grey slate roofs with lead flashings, cast-iron rain gutters, and matching stone chimney pots.
- **Civic Surfaces**: Basalt and whinstone sett detailing, smooth Caithness flagstone and sandstone pavement slabs bordered by heavy Craigleith granite/sandstone kerbstones, accompanied by wrought and cast-iron basement lightwell railings.

## 4. Landscape Ecology & Botanical Canopy
Urban mature deciduous trees punctuate private cathedral grounds, garden enclosures, and flanking avenue sidewalks. Botanical species include mature European sycamores (*Acer pseudoplatanus*), English oaks (*Quercus robur*), London plane trees (*Platanus x acerifolia*), and Wych elms (*Ulmus glabra*), rendered with full leafy canopies, distinct organic branch architecture, and healthy urban turfgrass borders within the cathedral perimeter grounds.

## 5. Static Civil Fabric Decluttering
All moving and parked cars along Palmerston Place, Manor Place, and adjacent crescents are completely purged. All transient human pedestrians, tourist crowds, wheeled waste bins, dumpsters, temporary construction hoardings, and street barricades are removed. Preserved static civil fixtures include cast-iron Victorian street lanterns, permanent perimeter stone walls, iron spears/railings, and stone kerbs.
