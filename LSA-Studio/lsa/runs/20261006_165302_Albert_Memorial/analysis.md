### STAGE 1: CENTROID IDENTIFICATION & REJECTION
The typical generative representation of St. Bernard’s Well defaults to an idyllic, sunlit Palladian folly with clean, uniform stone surfaces and clear, bright blue river water. This centroid is suppressed. The actual site is an intensely damp, deeply incised, semi-shaded incised river valley (the Dean Gorge) containing a late 18th-century structure built from local Carboniferous sandstone, marked by over two centuries of coal soot accumulation, hydraulic stress, and saturated riparian biofilms.

### STAGE 2 & 3: DOMAIN KEY EXCAVATION & CROSS-DOMAIN SYNERGY

#### 1. Geology & Petrology (Craigleith Sandstone & Dolerite)
*   **Substrate Composition:** The superstructure is largely constructed from fine-grained Craigleith Sandstone (quartz grains bonded by secondary silica with subsidiary clay and feldspar). When dry, it is pale cream to greyish-buff; under saturated conditions (post-rain), its refractive index alters, darkening to an earthy olive-drab and charcoal slate.
*   **Podium / Foundations:** The lower river-wall foundations incorporate denser, irregular ashlar and rusticated blockwork resting directly on river-cut shales and basaltic dolerite bedrock outcroppings of the Water of Leith basin. Capillary action (rising damp) constantly supersaturates the bottom 1.5 meters of stone blocks.

#### 2. Atmospheric Chemistry & Carbonaceous Weathering
*   **The "Auld Reekie" Legacy:** Sub-microscopic coal fly-ash and historical sulfur dioxide deposits have combined with calcium ions to form thick, black gypsum-carbon crusts (*scialbatura* alterations) lodged into sheltered architectural undercuts—beneath the Doric frieze, abaci, and the rusticated voussoirs of the lower well chamber arch.
*   **Runoff & Sulfate Streaking:** Horizontal surfaces wash clean, while sheltered vertical recesses feature dark, streaked soot veils that run vertically down the ESE stone faces where rain drainage tracks follow gravity.

#### 3. Masonry & Chemical Degradation
*   **Lime Leaching & Efflorescence:** Historical lime mortars have suffered calcite leaching (calcium carbonate dissolution and re-precipitation), leaving white, stalactitic mineral tracks along vertical bedding planes.
*   **Ferrous Expansion & Spalling:** Wrought iron railings set into the river retaining wall and temple periphery display severe sub-surface oxidation. The volumetric expansion of rusting iron has caused mechanical jacking, producing vertical micro-fractures and wedge spalls at the stone mortise pockets, bleeding orange ferric oxide stains down the wet masonry.

#### 4. Riparian Ecology & Bryophyte Biofilms
*   **Bryophyte Stratification:** The proximity to the slow-churning river creates a 90%+ local humidity zone. The lower ashlar blocks and rusticated water-level bases are colonized by dense cushions of aquatic/semi-aquatic mosses (*Rhynchostegium riparioides* and *Cinclidotus fontinaloides*), dark green and dripping.
*   **Algal Slimes & Micro-Flora:** Vertical stone faces pointing East-South-East, shielded from direct sun by the steep gorge walls and mature canopy, are coated with an emerald-green photosynthetic film of unicellular chlorophyte algae (*Desmococcus olivaceus*) and gelatinous patches of *Nostoc* cyanobacteria.
*   **Foliage Litter:** Decomposing, tannin-stained leaves of Sycamore (*Acer pseudoplatanus*) and European Beech (*Fagus sylvatica*) lie flattened and stuck like wet parchment against the damp horizontal stone copings and flagstone path.

### STAGE 4: DOMAIN CONFLICT RESOLUTION
*   **Conflict:** Neoclassical formal geometry dictates crisp, planar, classical lines, whereas an active river gorge causes aggressive organic disruption.
*   **Resolution:** The architecture retains its crisp geometric outline from Alexander Nasmyth’s 1789 design (slender Doric peristyle encircling the statue of Hygieia above), but the material reality at 4 meters is entirely dominated by surface breakdown: the crisp moldings are softened by water rounding, edges are broken by spalling, and the monumental podium blends into the riverbank via heavy moss blankets and river debris lines.
